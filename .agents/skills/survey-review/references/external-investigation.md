# External investigation: search and source access

Read this protocol before external searching, source retrieval or version checking. It supplies source evidence and search provenance to the relevant branch in contribution-investigation.md. It does not replace the academic judgment in that file.

## 1. Decide when external work is required

| Question | Manuscript-only work may settle | External evidence is required for |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge-increment identification | Whether the text forms a cross-study understanding or organization rather than a list or single-study restatement | Whether a prior account already supplies the same result is a separate novelty question |
| Correctness | Internal arithmetic, direct inconsistency, supplied definitions and whether stated premises logically support the conclusion | Fidelity to cited studies, external facts, source conditions or possible counterexamples |
| Relative novelty | What the manuscript says it adds and what its own cited accounts state | Whether prior work already expresses the same or overlapping understanding by the cutoff |
| Scholarly value | A bounded use argument directly supported by the artifact | Field importance, demonstrated benefits, reader needs or expert consensus when claimed |
| Expected knowledge | Whether the manuscript explicitly promises a concrete result | Whether an independent field expectation is real, important and within this survey's scope |

Do not perform a ceremonial search when the manuscript itself establishes a contradiction. Do not claim an external source is false or absent when the needed material was not inspected.

## 2. Form a discriminating search

Before searching, state the precise proposition and what observation would support, refute or leave it unresolved.

- Correctness: identify the source fact, definition, quantity, condition or inference that needs verification.
- Relative novelty: express the candidate as a concrete relation, classification, explanation, assessment or research direction; search for prior accounts that supply the same understanding.
- Scholarly value: name the output, target use and claimed benefit; seek evidence for the underlying problem or reader task.
- Expected knowledge: identify the trigger, survey scope and proposed response; search for grounds that the expectation exists and matters.

Adapt queries to results. Use exact titles and identifiers for source identity, alternate terms for conceptual matches, and citation links for expansion. Do not require a fixed keyword sequence, provider or API key.

## 3. Retrieve and qualify sources

1. Use available scholarly or general search, repository pages, publisher pages, cited originals or legitimate author copies.
2. Prefer the original study for its methods and findings; use a survey to establish what it synthesizes.
3. Confirm that the source addresses the same proposition and conditions. Topic overlap alone is not a match.
4. Identify the substantive version and its availability date. Confirm that it was available by the unchanged review cutoff. Access date does not establish historical availability.
5. Inspect the decisive passage. Search snippets and abstracts can help discover a source; they cannot verify unobserved methods, results or comparison logic.
6. Create source evidence only for material actually inspected. Record identity, version, date, locator, excerpt and relevance.
7. If the version, date or decisive passage cannot be checked, retain an unknown or partial assessment.
8. Exclude post-cutoff evidence from historical novelty and obligation judgments. A later source may point to a potentially eligible earlier work, which must then be independently retrieved and checked.

## 4. Seek contrary evidence

For central claims and expected omissions, inspect relevant support, author qualifications, plausible counterexamples and competing explanations. Search for a concrete discriminator when one exists. Do not invent an alternative without a factual or logical basis.

For novelty, inspect the closest pre-cutoff account even when it weakens the candidate. Compare substantive passages, not only titles. For value, test whether the candidate actually supports the claimed use and what conditions limit it. Keep support and contrary passages; do not decide by source count.

## 5. Stop with a bounded conclusion

| Situation | Allowed conclusion |
|---|---|
| Direct sources and relevant contrary evidence inspected; no unresolved discriminator could change the stated result | A scoped assessment with explicit remaining coverage limits |
| A prior account supplies the same specific understanding | The named overlap exists; assess any remaining difference separately |
| No matching account returned after recorded searches | No match was located within the recorded search; global novelty remains unknown |
| A credible problem and a candidate's response are established | A reasoned value assessment with conditions; not measured benefit or consensus unless those were measured |
| A supporting passage found but a feasible, decisive contrary check remains | Keep the assessment provisional and investigate the discriminator |
| Critical source/version is unavailable or conflict remains after bounded attempts | Unknown or limited assessment with actual attempts and missing evidence |

Search sufficiency is a reasoned stopping decision about the stated proposition, not a source quota. A failed attempt can end one iteration; it does not settle the proposition.

## 6. Update the run

After actual search/access work:

1. Record the real attempt as a search event, including failures and no-result outcomes.
2. Record inspected passages as source evidence.
3. Update the relevant candidate, expectation and assessment records.
4. Update an impact record only when evidence establishes an effect on scholarly use.
5. Record the stopping reason and remaining unknown in the completed step.
6. Keep the cutoff unchanged and re-sort the investigation agenda.
